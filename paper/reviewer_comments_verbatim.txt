REVIEWER 1
================================================================================

The paper evaluates five foundation LLMs for AML screening, finding false-positive
rates ranging from 0.3% to 83.0%.

Results are reported accurately with appropriate statistical measures.

Conclusions are well-founded with clear caveats about synthetic data limitations and
hypothetical projections.

I recommend that the manuscript be accepted. The study is methodologically sound,
results are reported accurately, and conclusions are appropriate.

================================================================================
REVIEWER 2 - COVER COMMENTS
================================================================================

The paper asks whether the choice of commercial foundation model, rather than the
screening task or prompt, materially changes the operating point of an LLM-based AML
transaction screener. The authors run five zero-shot foundation models from two
providers (three OpenAI, two Google) on an identical, preregistered battery of 600
synthetic transaction cases (300 suspicious across eight FATF-anchored typologies,
300 legitimate "hard negative" cases), crossed with two prompt formulations and two
random seeds (12,000 calls total). They report a large spread in false-positive rate
across models (0.3%-83.0%), a monotonic within-provider relationship between model
tier and false-positive rate, high cross-model disagreement on legitimate cases
(85.7%), low chance-corrected agreement on misses, and an illustrative ~197-fold
difference in projected daily alert volume under a low-prevalence scenario. The paper
frames these findings as a model-risk/governance issue rather than a model-capability
issue, and supplies a full reproducibility package.

This is a carefully designed and quite transparent empirical study. The core idea -
treating foundation-model identity as the independent variable while holding the task
and cases fixed, and reframing the result in model-risk-governance terms - is a useful
contribution to a literature that often focuses on identifying the best model. The
statistical treatment is appropriate for the paired design, and the reproducibility
package is appreciated.

I recommend minor revisions, focused mainly on (a) sharpening the originality claim
(b) clarifying a few methodological choices that affect the headline numbers, and
(c) some robustness check.

Please find the comments do be addressed in the uploaded file.

REVIEWER 2 - ATTACHMENT, COMMENT 1 (novelty claim)
================================================================================

The central claim is credible but should be stated more precisely. The authors state
they are "the first study to control foundation-model identity as the experimental
variable in AML transaction screening while fixing the task and transaction cases".
Within AML specifically this is plausible and, to my knowledge, not previously done in
this controlled way. However, the general phenomenon being measured - that different
LLMs disagree substantially on identical inputs, and that prompt-induced variance is
smaller than model-induced variance - has precedent in the broader LLM-evaluation
literature. I would ask the authors to sharpen the novelty claim to "first
domain-specific, governance-framed controlled measurement of this effect in AML
screening" rather than implying the phenomenon itself is new.

================================================================================
REVIEWER 2 - ATTACHMENT, COMMENT 2 (governance reframing)
================================================================================

The governance reframing is the paper's strongest and most original contribution.
Treating model-tier substitution and provider-managed '-latest' aliases as first-class
model-risk events, and proposing a standing "operating-point probe" as a monitoring
control, is a useful and new operationalisation for this domain. This contribution
stands on its own even if the underlying "models disagree a lot" finding were less
novel, and I'd encourage the authors to foreground it even more.

================================================================================
REVIEWER 2 - ATTACHMENT, COMMENT 3 (provider coverage)
================================================================================

A natural extension that bears on originality. The study covers only OpenAI and Google
models, while the Generative AI declaration notes that Anthropic Claude was used for
coding assistance. Given the paper's thesis is about provider dependence and model-swap
risk, omitting other providers leaves the "provider effect vs. tier effect" distinction
slightly underdetermined, a sentence explaining the provider selection (cost, access,
or scope reasons) would help.

================================================================================
REVIEWER 2 - ATTACHMENT, COMMENT 4 (construct validity of the battery)
================================================================================

Construct validity of the case battery. The false-positive/miss-rate estimates rest
entirely on the correctness of the synthetic labels. The Methods section describes how
cases were generated but does not state whether the "legitimate hard negative" and
"suspicious" labels were reviewed or validated by an independent AML domain expert.
I'd ask the authors to clarify whether any expert adjudication or inter-rater check was
performed on a sample of the battery, and if not, to say so explicitly as a limitation.

================================================================================
REVIEWER 2 - ATTACHMENT, COMMENT 5 (tie-breaking rule, decision D4)
================================================================================

The tie-breaking rule in the aggregation procedure (Appendix A, decision D4) deserves
more scrutiny. The rule resolves 2-2 replicate splits "with reference to the known case
label" (a tie counts as not a false positive for benign cases, and counted as caught
for suspicious cases). This rule affects 132/1500 (8.8%) of benign and 48/1500 (3.2%)
of suspicious model-case decisions, which is not negligible relative to some of the
reported rates. I would ask for:

a breakdown of how many ties occurred per model, since if ties cluster in particular
models, the label-referenced tie-break could differentially inflate or deflate that
model's reported rate;

a sensitivity analysis reporting the headline spread (82.7 pp) and the cross-model
Q-test under an alternative, label-agnostic tie rule, to show the qualitative
conclusion is not an artifact of D4.

================================================================================
REVIEWER 2 - ATTACHMENT, COMMENT 6 (statistical independence)
================================================================================

Statistical independence assumptions. McNemar's test and Cochran's Q assume the 300
legitimate cases are exchangeable/independent units. Since cases are synthetically
generated from a shared typology structure, some correlation across cases seems
plausible. The authors use cluster bootstrapping over the eight typology strata for the
secondary uncertainty estimates but not, as far as I can tell, for the primary
McNemar/Cochran tests. Given how strong the effect is (P = 2x10^-27), this is unlikely
to change the qualitative conclusion, but a brief comment on why case-level inference
is appropriate for the primary test would be useful.

================================================================================
REVIEWER 2 - ATTACHMENT, COMMENT 7 (monotonicity)
================================================================================

"Model tier" is treated ordinally but tested only descriptively. The paper observes a
monotonic FPR decrease with tier within each provider and calls this a "consistent
pattern,". I'd suggest adding a formal trend test to support the word "monotonic," or
otherwise soften "monotonic" to "monotonically decreasing in the two families studied"
(which is closer to what is actually shown with only 2-3 tiers per provider).

================================================================================
REVIEWER 2 - ATTACHMENT, COMMENT 8 (prevalence sensitivity)
================================================================================

Operational projection is a single point, not a curve. The 197-fold alert-volume claim
rests on one prevalence assumption (0.1%) and one volume assumption (1,000,000/day).
The paper is appropriately cautious that this is illustrative, but since alert volume
and precision both scale nonlinearly with prevalence, a supplementary figure sweeping
prevalence over a plausible range (e.g., 0.01%-1%) would let readers see whether the
relative ordering of models (and the ~200-fold spread) is stable across the plausible
range, or whether it is sensitive to the specific 0.1% choice.

================================================================================
REVIEWER 2 - ATTACHMENT, COMMENT 9 (zero-shot scope)
================================================================================

Zero-shot-only scope. This is a deliberate and well-justified design choice (isolating
the base-model contribution before task-specific tuning), and the limitation is
honestly stated. No change needed, but I'd suggest one added sentence in the Discussion
speculating on whether calibration/threshold-tuning would be expected to compress the
cross-model spread (since institutions could in principle recalibrate each model to a
common operating point) or whether the paper's evidence (e.g., the persistence of
ordering across two prompts) suggests the divergence is more structural than a simple
decision-threshold shift. This matters for how alarming the governance implication
should be considered.

================================================================================
REVIEWER 2 - ATTACHMENT, MINOR POINT 10 (definition of model tier)
================================================================================

Please define "model tier" operationally in the Methods (currently it is defined via
"provider's contemporaneous capability/product and price positioning," which is
reasonable but could usefully cite the specific list-price/capability source alongside
the models.yaml registry reference).

================================================================================
REVIEWER 2 - ATTACHMENT, MINOR POINT 11 (confidence interval in the abstract)
================================================================================

The abstract states "an 82.7 percentage-point spread" while the Results also report a
95% CI of 69.2-95.3 pp for that spread; consider including the CI in the abstract as
well, since a >25-point CI width is itself informative.

================================================================================
REVIEWER 2 - ATTACHMENT, MINOR POINT 12 (scope of the headline language)
================================================================================

Consider explicitly stating in the Limitations that only two providers/five models were
tested, so the paper's own headline "foundation-model choice" language should be read as
scoped to the evaluated model set, not as a universal claim about all foundation models
(the paper mostly does this correctly, but the Conclusion's phrasing is slightly more
general than the evidence supports).

================================================================================
REVIEWER 2 - ATTACHMENT, MINOR POINT 13 (typography and formatting)
================================================================================

There are quite many typos such as numbers inserted across brackets in many paragraphs.
Formatting also could be improved.

================================================================================
REVIEWER 2 - RECOMMENDATION
================================================================================

Minor revision. The core empirical contribution - a controlled, paired, preregistered
demonstration that foundation-model identity alone produces large, statistically robust
shifts in AML screening operating points, reframed as a model-risk governance issue -
is original and well-executed. The requested revisions are addressable without new data
collection, since the reproducibility package already contains everything needed to
rerun the relevant analyses under alternative assumptions.

REVIEWER 3
================================================================================

This manuscript investigates whether foundation-model choice materially changes the
operating point of LLM-based anti-money-laundering transaction screening when the task
and transaction cases are held constant. The topic is timely and the controlled
cross-model design, paired statistical testing, reproducibility framework, and
translation of false-positive rates into operational alert burden are valuable
strengths. However, several methodological issues should be addressed before the
conclusions can be fully supported, particularly the per-case aggregation procedure
used to combine replicate model decisions.

Major comment - label-dependent aggregation rule:

The preregistered aggregation rule resolves 2-2 replicate splits differently depending
on the known ground-truth label: a tie on a legitimate case is treated as legitimate,
whereas a tie on a suspicious case is treated as detected. Although this rule was
preregistered and transparently disclosed, it uses information that would not be
available in deployment and therefore may produce an optimistically biased evaluation
operating point. This is especially important because ties affected a non-trivial
number of model-case decisions. I recommend reporting the primary results under a
label-independent aggregation rule and providing sensitivity analyses under alternative
tie policies, such as treating ties as abstentions, always escalating ties, or
analysing each prompt configuration separately. The authors should also clarify whether
the headline false-positive and miss rates represent deployable model configurations or
an evaluation-only aggregation procedure.
